The business wants to know *how many* units of an item are bought per order.
The orders team adds a column `quantity` to `line_items`, defaulting to 1.
Harmless? Not for a pipeline that expects exactly three columns, e.g. a `SELECT *` into a fixed target table.
So you release the change as a **new major version**: `orders_v2`, in its own database schema, next to `orders_v1`.

<Goals>
- Release a change as a new major version of a data contract
- Compare two versions with `datacontract changelog` and `datacontract breaking`
- Understand what counts as a breaking change, and where tools reach their limits
- Announce removals early with the `deprecated` flag (new in ODCS 3.2)
- Walk through the contract lifecycle: draft → active → deprecated → retired
</Goals>

<Callout type="concept" title="Breaking vs. non-breaking changes">
A change is **breaking** when a consumer that worked yesterday can fail today without changing anything. Typical breaking changes:

- **removing or renaming** a field or table
- **changing a type**, e.g. `BIGINT` → `TEXT`
- **weakening a guarantee**, e.g. a `required` field becomes optional, or a quality rule is relaxed

Adding an optional field, improving descriptions, or adding quality checks are typically **non-breaking**.

Contracts use **semantic versioning**: breaking changes bump the **major** version (`1.x` → `2.0.0`), compatible additions the minor version, fixes the patch version.
</Callout>

<Callout type="concept" title="Tools see schemas, not consumers">
`datacontract breaking` says adding `quantity` is fine. Why a new major version anyway?

Because "breaking" depends on how consumers use the data. A consumer that loads `SELECT *` into a strict table, or validates the exact column list, *will* break. The tool checks compatibility rules. The producer still has to know their consumers, and releasing a major version is a deliberate, conservative decision.

[Consumer-Driven Contracts](#/en/consumer-driven) shows how consumers make their actual dependencies explicit.
</Callout>

## Execute the migration

<Callout type="concept" title="The contract lifecycle">
A contract version goes through these states:

1. **`draft`**: work in progress, don't build on it
2. **`active`**: released, consumers can rely on it
3. **`deprecated`**: still served, consumers should migrate; add an `endOfSupport` SLA property with a date
4. **`retired`**: no longer served

A full migration: set v2 to `active`, deprecate v1 with an end-of-support date, announce it in the support channel, and retire v1 once nobody queries it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="concept" title="Deprecating single fields (new in ODCS 3.2)">
Not every change needs a new major version right away. With `deprecated: true` on a schema object or property, you announce: "this field goes away in the next major version, stop building on it."
The field stays documented and tested, so existing consumers keep working. Removing it later is still a breaking change and needs the next major version.
</Callout>

<Quiz
  question="Which change to an active contract is breaking for consumers?"
  options={["Adding a new optional column", "Changing a column's physical type from BIGINT to TEXT", "Adding a description to a column"]}
  answer={1}
>
A type change breaks every consumer that relies on the old type; `datacontract breaking` reports it as ERROR. Optional columns and descriptions are schema-compatible (though your consumers might still disagree).
